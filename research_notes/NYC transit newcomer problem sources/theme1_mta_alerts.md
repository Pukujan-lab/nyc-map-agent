# Theme 1: Why MTA service-change and disruption notices are hard to understand, and plain-language / accessibility standards for transit alerts

Verification tags in each claim: [fetched] = I opened the page and read the relevant text; [snippet] = URL and claim came from a search result summary only, not opened (treat as lower confidence). Web search budget ran out mid-task, so some planned queries were not run (see Gaps).

## Q1. Evidence that MTA alerts and service-change notices are jargon-heavy, confusing, or inconsistent

### Takeaway
There is a long, consistent record (2008 to 2021) from the MTA's own rider councils, State/City Comptroller audits and news that service-change signs and notices are dense, shorthand-heavy, placed late or in the wrong place, and inconsistent; the MTA itself launched a survey in 2021 and a new alert system in 2023 to simplify wording. I found no independent post-2023 audit measuring whether the fixes worked, and the MTA's own 2026 claims are self-reported.

### Inferences
- Complaints recur across 15+ years (2008, 2009, 2010, 2011, 2014, 2021, 2023) with the same themes (late or hidden signs, shorthand, too much text, inconsistent direction wording), which suggests a structural problem rather than a one-off.
- Because the MTA itself said in 2021 that it did not know which direction terms work best, plain-language translation plus a map is aimed at a gap the agency has acknowledged.
- The NYC Comptroller 2017 grade (85% "C" or lower) concerns on-train announcements, not written notices; it is related evidence, not direct evidence on service-change notices.

### Gaps
- No dedicated Riders Alliance, Straphangers Campaign, Tri-State Transportation Campaign or TransitCenter report on service-change notice clarity surfaced; only a Riders Alliance funding campaign page (https://transitcenter.org/built-to-win-riders-alliance-campaign-gets-mta-to-fund-more-frequent-service/ [snippet]) which is not about notices.

- No quantitative post-2023 measure of rider comprehension of the new alert system.

## Q3. Plain-language laws and standards (Plain Writing Act, NYC rules, WCAG, ADA, FTA, GTFS-realtime)

### Takeaway
The federal Plain Writing Act binds federal agencies (not the MTA, a state authority), but its guidelines (active voice, ~20-word sentences) are the common reference; NYC Local Law 30 (2017) mandates translation into 10 languages for city agencies; the MTA has its own Title VI/LEP duty that includes translating service changes; WCAG 3.1.5 (AAA) sets a lower-secondary reading level; GTFS-realtime provides the structured fields (cause, effect, header, description) for alerts.

### Inferences
- The Plain Writing Act does not legally cover the MTA; it is a benchmark, not a mandate. Anyone stating the MTA "must" follow it would be wrong based on what I found (MTA status as non-federal entity is my inference, not a sourced statement).
- WCAG 3.1.5 is AAA, so it is aspirational, not typically a required conformance level; writing at about a 9th-grade level or lower with a map and icon support meets the criterion's own sufficient techniques (summary, illustrations, spoken version).
- The MTA's LEP plan commits to translating service changes but names only Spanish, so multilingual coverage of alerts is a gap an app could fill.

- Did not find the FTA ADA circular text on information access; source above is only the Federal Register notice.

## Q4. Research on alert design, real-time information and rider anxiety / perceived wait time

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed work shows real-time information reduces perceived wait (Seattle: about 13%) and anxiety/frustration (Tampa), and UK evidence says riders in disruption most want cause, alternatives and honesty; practitioner guidance says lead with effect, keep it short, and structure data. No controlled study of alert wording comprehension was found.

### Inferences

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found on comprehension of transit alert wording or jargon, in NYC or elsewhere.
- No NYC-specific study on rider anxiety during planned service changes.
- Several PDFs (Brakewood Tampa, DfT, ORR) could not be read through the fetch tool; their figures come from search summaries and should be verified against the originals before being quoted in the final problem statement.
